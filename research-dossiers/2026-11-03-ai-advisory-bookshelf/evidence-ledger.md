# Evidence ledger · AI advisory bookshelf
Access date throughout: 3 November 2026. Primary pages were read, not merely search snippets, unless marked unresolved. Fact about a vendor's documented offering does not mean tested efficacy. Confidence concerns the stated narrow claim. Research-map.md supplies proposed tests, not collected results. No material public claim should exceed this ledger.

## E01 · People ask Claude for personal guidance; sycophancy remains a measured issue
- Type: fact about reported observational classification; confidence high for report, moderate for population generalisation.
- Direct source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-personal-guidance ; published 30 April 2026, March–April 2026 sample. Read methods and summary.
- Claim: roughly 6% of analysed conversations sought personal guidance; professional/career was 26% of guidance; sycophantic behaviour appeared in 9% of guidance chats. Initial random million conversations were filtered to roughly 639,000 unique-user conversations, yielding about 38,000 guidance conversations.
- Proves: advice-seeking is observable in this provider's sample and the lab is working on response behaviour.
- Does not prove: all AI advice frequency, founder demand, paid bookshelf demand, harm rate or that retrieval reduces sycophancy.
- Counterevidence: lab reports model training improvements; a separate layer competes with improving defaults. Taxonomy excludes general objective information.
- Decision impact: motivation for a quality test; never multiply the percentages into startup revenue.

## E02 · ChatGPT practical guidance is broad and about 29%, not a personal-coaching market share
- Type: fact about study estimate; confidence high for reported taxonomy/share.
- Direct source: https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/a253471f-8260-40c6-a2cc-aa93fe9f142e/economic-research-chatgpt-usage-paper.pdf ; 15 September 2025, Table 3/section 5.2/Figures 7 and 9 (PDF pages 14–17).
- Claim: text reports practical guidance at roughly 29% of consumer usage; includes how-to, tutoring, ideation and health/fitness/self-care. Sample covers consumer plans, approximately 1.1 million sampled conversations May 2024–June 2025; plotted shares concern user messages.
- Proves: broad customised guidance use; distinct denominator/taxonomy from E01.
- Does not prove: exact supplied 28.8% decimal (not verified in text), executive coaching share, enterprise usage or new subscription demand.
- Counterevidence: tutoring and generic how-to are large components; dated sample and automated classifications.
- Decision impact: use “roughly 29%” only; no cross-provider percentage comparison chart implying equivalence.

## E11 · ShadowBox offers evidence for structured case/rationale comparison, not this product
- Type: fact about primary study/method; confidence high for reported study, low for startup transfer.
- Direct sources: https://www.shadowboxtraining.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/The_ShadowBox_Approach_to_Cognitive_Skills_Trainin.pdf and https://doi.org/10.1177/1555343416636515 ; Klein & Borders, March 2016, method pp1–2 and limitations pp9–10 (printed).
- Claim: learners commit rankings/rationales before comparing expert rationale; unresolved minority views can remain. Two military evaluations reported 28% and 21% improvement in alignment with expert rankings, N=59 and N=30 respectively.
- Proves: this study's training results and a plausible design inspiration.
- Does not prove: better founder decisions, skill transfer, field performance or benefit of passively reading excerpts. Authors explicitly say job-performance transfer is uncertain; rationale was not systematically included in score.
- Counterevidence: static scenarios/preselected options and expert-agreement scoring limit generalisation.
- Decision impact: measure decisions/retention separately; don't advertise “21–28% better advice”.
